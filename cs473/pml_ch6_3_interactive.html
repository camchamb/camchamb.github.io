PML • Chapter 6.3

Mutual Information

Scope: 6.3.1–6.3.5, 6.3.8, 6.3.9. Skipped: 6.3.6, 6.3.7, and 6.3.10.

Goal: Be able to explain what mutual information measures, calculate it, interpret its value, distinguish it from correlation, and understand the data-processing and sufficient-statistic ideas.

1. The big idea

Mutual information (MI) measures how much knowing one random variable tells us about another. It is zero exactly when the variables are independent (for the usual discrete setting).

I(X;Y) = Σₓ Σᵧ p(x,y) log [ p(x,y) / (p(x)p(y)) ]

Equivalent views:

  • KL view: I(X;Y) = KL(p(x,y) || p(x)p(y)).
  • Entropy view: I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y) = H(Y) − H(Y|X).
Intuition: Start with uncertainty about X. After observing Y, some uncertainty may disappear. MI is the average amount that disappears.

3. Why MI is a KL divergence

I(X;Y) = KL( p(X,Y) || p(X)p(Y) )

The first distribution is the actual joint distribution. The second is what the joint distribution would look like if X and Y were independent but kept their same marginals.

So MI answers:

“How different is the actual joint distribution from the independence assumption?”

Because KL divergence is nonnegative, MI ≥ 0. Also, MI is symmetric:

I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)

4. MI as reduction in uncertainty

Before observing Y

H(X)

After observing Y

H(X|Y)

Information gained

I(X;Y)

The identity is:

I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y)

If Y tells you nothing about X, then H(X|Y)=H(X), so MI=0. If Y completely determines X, then H(X|Y)=0, so MI=H(X).

5. Example: independent vs. dependent variables

For binary variables, perfect dependence gives 1 bit of MI when X is balanced. Notice that MI does not care whether the relationship is “positive” or “negative”; both can carry information.

Key example: If Y=X² and X is symmetric around 0, correlation can be 0 even though Y is completely determined by X. MI detects that dependence.

7. Conditional mutual information

Sometimes X and Y appear related only because of a third variable Z. Conditional MI asks how much information X and Y share after Z is known.

I(X;Y | Z) = H(X|Z) − H(X|Y,Z)

It can also be viewed as an average of MI values within each condition Z=z:

I(X;Y|Z) = Σ_z p(z) I(X;Y | Z=z)

Important: conditioning can change the apparent relationship. Two variables can have high marginal dependence but much less dependence after conditioning on Z.

8. Data processing inequality

If information flows through a Markov chain

X → Y → Z

then processing Y into Z cannot create new information about X:

I(X;Z) ≤ I(X;Y)
Intuition: Z is produced from Y. Once Y is known, Z cannot contain information about X that wasn't already available through Y.

Original signal

Think: lots of information about X.

Processed signal

At most as much information about X.

9. Sufficient statistics

A statistic S(X) is sufficient for a parameter θ if, once S(X) is known, the remaining details of X provide no additional information about θ.

θ → S(X) → X

Equivalently, the data can be compressed into S without losing information relevant to inference about θ.

Example: For N Bernoulli trials, the total number of successes is sufficient for the success probability μ. You do not need to remember the order of heads and tails.

This connects directly to information theory: a good sufficient statistic preserves the information about the parameter that matters for inference.

10. Concept map

  • MI: information shared by X and Y.
  • Entropy: uncertainty in a variable.
  • Conditional entropy: uncertainty remaining after observing another variable.
  • MI = entropy reduction: H(X)−H(X|Y).
  • MI = KL from independence: KL(p(x,y)||p(x)p(y)).
  • Conditional MI: shared information after conditioning on Z.
  • Data processing inequality: processing cannot increase information about the original variable.
  • Sufficient statistic: compression that preserves information needed about a parameter.
