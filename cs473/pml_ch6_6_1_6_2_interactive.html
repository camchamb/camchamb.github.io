Big picture

What is Chapter 6 trying to teach?

Information theory gives us ways to quantify uncertainty, surprise, and how different two probability distributions are.

Entropy H(P)

How uncertain is a random variable? Higher entropy means the probability mass is spread out more.

Cross-entropy H(P,Q)

How costly is it to encode outcomes from P using Q?

KL divergence KL(P‖Q)

How inefficient is Q relative to P, after removing P's own entropy?

Perplexity

A convenient exponential form of cross-entropy, especially common for language models.

6.1 — Entropy

H(X) = − Σₓ p(x) log p(x)

Think of entropy as the expected surprise of observing X. If an outcome has probability p, its surprise is −log p.

Key intuition: A fair coin is more uncertain than a coin that lands heads 99% of the time. Therefore the fair coin has higher entropy.

Units depend on the log

  • log₂ → bits
  • ln → nats

6.1.3–6.1.5 — Joint, conditional entropy, and perplexity

H(X,Y) = −ΣₓΣᵧ p(x,y) log p(x,y)
H(Y|X) = H(X,Y) − H(X)

Conditional entropy asks: How much uncertainty remains about Y after I know X?

Perplexity = exp(H)   (when H uses natural logs)

Entropy and conditional entropy are about uncertainty in a distribution; KL divergence is about the mismatch between two distributions.

6.2 — Relative entropy / KL divergence

KL(P‖Q) = Σₓ p(x) log [p(x) / q(x)]

KL divergence measures how much information is lost when Q is used to represent P.

  • KL(P‖Q) ≥ 0
  • KL(P‖Q) = 0 iff P = Q (on the relevant support)
  • It is not symmetric: KL(P‖Q) ≠ KL(Q‖P) in general.
Important: KL divergence is called a “divergence,” not a distance. It does not generally satisfy symmetry or the triangle inequality.

Why does this matter for ML?

When the true labels are represented by a distribution P and your model predicts Q, minimizing cross-entropy pushes Q toward P. This is why cross-entropy is so common as a classification loss.

Three rules to memorize

  1. KL is always nonnegative.
  2. KL is zero only when the distributions match.
  3. Order matters. KL(P‖Q) and KL(Q‖P) generally differ.
6.2.6 — Important

Forward KL vs. Reverse KL

The direction of KL changes what the optimization “cares about.” This is one of the most important conceptual ideas in Section 6.2.6.

Forward KL

KL(P‖Q)

Expectation is under P. If P puts probability somewhere, Q is strongly penalized for putting too little probability there.

Mode-covering intuition: Q tends to spread enough mass to cover the modes of P.

Reverse KL

KL(Q‖P)

Expectation is under Q. Q is heavily penalized for placing mass where P says probability is tiny or zero.

Mode-seeking intuition: Q can concentrate on one high-probability region rather than covering every mode.

Interactive intuition

Imagine P has two separated peaks, while Q is a simpler single-peaked distribution. Toggle the objective below.

Why the difference?

KL(P‖Q) = EP[log P(X) − log Q(X)]

Forward KL samples where P has mass, so missing an important region of P is expensive.

KL(Q‖P) = EQ[log Q(X) − log P(X)]

Reverse KL samples where Q puts mass, so Q avoids regions where P is very small. That creates the mode-seeking behavior.

Exam-level takeaway: Do not merely memorize “forward = covering, reverse = seeking.” Be able to explain it from which distribution supplies the expectation.
