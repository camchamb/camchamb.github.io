1. The big picture

Section 3.1 moves from one random variable to multiple random variables together. The key object is a joint distribution such as p(X,Y).

p(X,Y) → covariance/correlation → dependence structure

Section 3.2 specializes this to the multivariate Gaussian (MVN), which describes a Gaussian-shaped cloud in multiple dimensions.

Joint distribution

Describes probabilities/densities for variables occurring together.

Covariance

Measures how two variables vary together. Sign gives direction; magnitude depends on units.

Correlation

Standardized covariance, always between −1 and +1.

Independence

Knowing one variable tells you nothing about the other: p(X,Y)=p(X)p(Y).

2. Covariance vs. correlation

Cov(X,Y)=E[(X−μX)(Y−μY)]
Corr(X,Y)=Cov(X,Y)/(σXσY)

Intuition: imagine centered X and Y. If they tend to be above their means at the same time, covariance is positive. If one tends to be above while the other is below, it is negative.

Important: zero covariance does not generally imply independence. For jointly Gaussian variables, however, zero covariance does imply independence.

3. Multivariate Gaussian

x ~ N(μ, Σ)

Here μ is the mean vector and Σ is the covariance matrix. The covariance matrix controls the size, orientation, and shape of the Gaussian cloud.

ObjectWhat it tells you
μCenter of the distribution
Diagonal of ΣVariances of individual dimensions
Off-diagonal of ΣCovariances between dimensions
Σ⁻¹Appears in the Mahalanobis distance
dM(x)=√((x−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−μ))

Unlike ordinary Euclidean distance, Mahalanobis distance accounts for the distribution's scale and correlation.

What should you notice?

  • Positive correlation → upward-sloping cloud.
  • Negative correlation → downward-sloping cloud.
  • Near zero correlation → no linear trend.
  • Correlation describes linear association, not causation.
