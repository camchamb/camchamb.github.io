17.3.5 Turning SVM scores into probabilities

An SVM outputs a score f(x), not a probability. Platt scaling fits p(y=1|f)=σ(A·f+B) on held-out scores. Orange = class 1, blue = class 0.

Why it works: the SVM only cares about points near the margin, so f is a signed distance-like quantity with an arbitrary scale. A logistic map on top fixes the scale (A) and the offset (B). Fit it on data the SVM was not trained on: on training data the support vectors sit at |f|≈1 and the scores look overconfident.

17.3.6 SVM vs. logistic regression: the loss view

With margin m = y·f(x), the SVM minimises the hinge loss (plus ‖w‖²) and logistic regression minimises log(1+e−m). Move the margin and compare.

Why it matters: both are convex upper bounds on the 0‑1 loss. The hinge is exactly zero once m≥1, so those points have zero gradient and drop out of the solution. That is what makes the SVM solution sparse in the dual (only support vectors have α>0). The logistic loss never reaches zero, so every point keeps pulling, and the result is naturally probabilistic.

17.3.7 Multi-class: one-vs-rest

Train K binary classifiers fk(x)=wk·x+b and see where they agree. Gray = no classifier fires, black hatch = several fire (ambiguous).

Why it fails: each fk is trained independently, so nothing forces exactly one to be positive. Argmax removes the holes, but the scores of different classifiers were never calibrated against each other (see 17.3.5), which is a reason to prefer probabilistic outputs or a proper multi-class loss.

Why: C weights the slack penalty. Small C tolerates margin violations, giving a wide margin, many support vectors and a smooth boundary (high bias). Large C punishes every violation, so the boundary bends around noisy points (high variance). γ interacts with C: a large γ makes the kernel local and more flexible, so the best C shifts. Pick them jointly by cross-validation, usually on a log grid as in the lower plot.

17.4 Sparse vector machines: the RVM

RVM = Bayesian linear model on RBF basis functions centred at every training point, with a separate Gaussian prior precision αi per weight (ARD). Evidence maximisation drives most αi→∞, which prunes those basis functions. Dashed = true sinc, solid = RVM mean ±2 s.d., green = dense kernel ridge regression, circles = relevance vectors.

Why it is sparse (17.4.1): for each weight, the marginal likelihood prefers αi=∞ unless the basis function explains something the others cannot. This automatic relevance determination is the Bayesian answer to the SVM’s hinge-loss sparsity. Comparison (17.4.2): RVMs are usually much sparser than SVMs and give predictive variances, but training is non-convex and costs O(M³) per iteration, and relevance vectors tend to sit away from the decision boundary, while SVM support vectors sit on it. Note how the band widens where no relevance vector is nearby.
