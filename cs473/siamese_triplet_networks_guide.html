Siamese & Triplet Networks

Build intuition for learning a useful embedding space: similar things should land close together, while dissimilar things should be separated. Explore the idea visually, adjust a triplet-loss example, and check your understanding.

1. The big idea: teach a model what “similar” means

Imagine a photo-search app. You upload a picture of your dog and want it to find other pictures of the same dog—not just pictures with the same background. A classifier predicts a label. A metric-learning model instead learns a representation (an embedding) where distance has meaning.

1. Siamese networks: pairwise learning

A Siamese architecture applies the same encoder \(f_\\theta\) to two inputs:

z₁ = fθ(x₁)  z₂ = fθ(x₂)

Then a distance or similarity function compares the embeddings. For Euclidean distance:

d(z₁,z₂) = ||z₁ − z₂||₂

Training examples can be labeled as similar (positive pair) or dissimilar (negative pair). A contrastive-style objective rewards small distances for positive pairs and penalizes negative pairs that are too close.

Lpair = y · d² + (1 − y) · max(0, m − d)²

Here y=1 means “similar,” y=0 means “dissimilar,” and m is a margin. This is one common contrastive-loss convention; other sources may swap label conventions or use slightly different formulas.

2. Triplet networks: relative comparisons

Instead of saying only whether a pair matches, give the model a three-item relationship:

  • Anchor (a): the reference example.
  • Positive (p): an example that should be similar to the anchor.
  • Negative (n): an example that should be less similar to the anchor.
Ltriplet = max(0, d(a,p)² − d(a,n)² + α)

The margin α says the negative should be farther away than the positive by at least a chosen gap. If the gap is already large enough, the loss is zero and that triplet gives no gradient under this loss.

The diagram indicates the desired relationship: d(a,p) should be smaller than d(a,n). It does not require every positive to be close to every other positive in every possible dataset.

3. Why shared weights?

If each branch had a different encoder, the model could map the anchor and positive using different coordinate systems. Shared weights ensure all inputs are embedded by the same function, making distances comparable. The branches are multiple uses of one encoder—not three independent models.

Training loop, conceptually

  1. Sample pairs or triplets from labeled data.
  2. Run each item through the shared encoder.
  3. Compute embedding distances and the loss.
  4. Backpropagate; update the shared encoder parameters.
  5. Repeat with useful, varied examples.

1. Triplet loss, step by step

For squared Euclidean distances, a common triplet objective is:

L(a,p,n) = max(0, ||f(a)−f(p)||² − ||f(a)−f(n)||² + α)

Define \(d_{ap}^2\) as the anchor-positive squared distance and \(d_{an}^2\) as the anchor-negative squared distance. The loss is positive when:

d²ₐₙ < d²ₐₚ + α

That means the negative is not sufficiently far away. The hinge-like max operation makes the loss zero once the required margin is satisfied.

3. Data sampling is part of the learning problem

Triplet loss can be unhelpful if most triplets are trivially easy. If every negative is already far away, the loss quickly becomes zero and contributes little learning signal. Common strategies include:

  • Random negatives: simple to sample, but many may be too easy.
  • Hard negatives: negatives close to the anchor in embedding space; informative but can be noisy or mislabeled.
  • Semi-hard negatives: negatives farther than the positive but still within the margin region, depending on the exact mining convention.
  • Balanced batches: include several examples from multiple identities/classes so useful comparisons can be formed.
Important caveat: hard-negative mining can backfire

If a “negative” is actually a valid match, or if labels are noisy, forcing it away teaches the wrong geometry. Very hard negatives can also destabilize training early. Mining strategy, batch construction, and data quality matter.

4. What the loss does—and does not—guarantee

  • It enforces local constraints for the pairs/triplets used during training.
  • It does not guarantee that every same-class example is closer than every different-class example.
  • The embedding geometry depends on the encoder, distance metric, margin, sampling strategy, and data.
  • At inference time, the encoder can embed a new item once; then compare it with stored embeddings, often without running a three-branch network.

One-minute summary

  • Siamese: shared encoder + pairwise comparison.
  • Triplet: shared encoder + anchor/positive/negative relationship.
  • Embedding: vector representation where distance is trained to carry meaning.
  • Triplet loss: penalizes cases where the negative is not farther than the positive by the margin.
  • Practical challenge: choose good pairs/triplets and mine informative negatives.
