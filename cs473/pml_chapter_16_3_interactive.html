Chapter 16.3: Kernel Density Estimation

Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction · Kevin P. Murphy

Three levels, an interactive density plot, a bandwidth experiment, and a quick quiz.

Level 1 · High-school friendly

What is a probability density?

Suppose you record the time students take to finish a quiz. You have a pile of measurements, but want to answer: What completion times are common, and which are unusual? Density estimation builds a smooth curve that describes where data tends to occur.

Histogram

Counts how many points land in each bin. The result can change a lot when you move the bin edges.

Kernel density estimate (KDE)

Place a small smooth bump at each observation, then add the bumps together.

Bandwidth

Controls each bump's width. Small width gives a wiggly curve; large width gives a smoother curve.

The core idea

estimated density at x = average of nearby smooth bumps centered on the data

Think of every measurement as ringing a tiny bell. Where many bells overlap, the estimated density is high. Where few overlap, it is low.

Remember: a density curve is not itself a probability at one exact point. Probability comes from the area under the curve across an interval.
Level 2 · Interactive exploration

See the bandwidth trade-off

The points below are a small sample. Change the bandwidth h and observe how the KDE changes. The estimate is a sum of Gaussian-shaped bumps, one centered at each sample.

Small bandwidth

Each observation has a narrow influence. Local detail is visible, but random noise can look like real structure.

Large bandwidth

Each observation influences a wider region. The estimate is smoother, but distinct groups can blur together.

A moderate bandwidth balances detail and smoothness.

Try to predict before moving the slider

What happens if the bandwidth becomes extremely small? You should see sharp narrow peaks around individual observations. What if it becomes very large? The bumps overlap broadly and the curve flattens into a broad shape.

Level 3 · College level

The KDE formula

Given observations \(x_1,\ldots,x_N\), a one-dimensional KDE is

p̂_h(x) = (1 / Nh) Σᵢ K((x − xᵢ) / h)
  • N: number of observations.
  • K: kernel function, often a Gaussian kernel.
  • h: positive bandwidth that sets the smoothing scale.

For a Gaussian kernel, \(K(u)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\exp(-u^2/2)\). Each data point contributes a Gaussian bump centered at \(x_i\); dividing by \(Nh\) gives the appropriate normalization in one dimension.

How to reason about the bias–variance trade-off

Too small h

Low smoothing bias but high variance: the curve reacts strongly to each sample and may overfit.

Too large h

More smoothing bias but often lower variance: important modes or gaps may disappear.

How is h chosen?

Bandwidth can be selected using cross-validation, plug-in rules, or domain knowledge. The best choice depends on the sample size, the shape of the true distribution, and the goal (visualization, density scoring, or downstream prediction).

What does “nonparametric” mean here?

KDE does not assume the data came from a fixed finite-dimensional family such as a Gaussian with only a mean and variance. The estimated shape can adapt to the data. KDE still has choices—kernel and bandwidth—and those choices matter.

How does KDE relate to K-nearest neighbors?

Both estimate local behavior from nearby observations. KDE uses a fixed distance scale (bandwidth) and smooth kernel weights; KNN methods commonly adapt the radius so that a fixed number of neighbors is included. The book moves from KDE toward KNN classification because local density ideas can support classification decisions.

Why does the area under the density equal one?

If K integrates to one, then each scaled kernel contributes total area 1/N. Summing N kernels yields total area one. The estimate is therefore a valid density (subject to the usual support/boundary considerations).

Recap

  • KDE estimates a distribution from samples without requiring a fixed parametric shape.
  • Each observation contributes a smooth kernel bump.
  • Bandwidth is the crucial smoothing choice.
  • Small bandwidth can overfit; large bandwidth can erase structure.
  • Probability is area under the density curve.
